# Positioning and messaging reference

Use this file when the task needs deeper work on strategic language, proof, voice, or a messaging document.

## Competitive alternatives

List what the customer could do without the brand:

- direct competitors
- indirect substitutes
- internal staff or manual work
- spreadsheets, agencies, tools, or informal workarounds
- delaying the decision or doing nothing

The status quo deserves explicit attention. A prospect may understand the offer and still decide that change is too costly or risky.

## Positioning worksheet

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who is the best-fit customer? | Specific role, industry, stage, geography, and trigger |
| What are they trying to change? | Current problem and desired outcome |
| What do they use instead? | Direct, indirect, internal, and inertial alternatives |
| What is genuinely different? | Observable attributes, not empty adjectives |
| Why does that difference matter? | Time, money, confidence, status, risk, or another outcome |
| What category should frame the offer? | The comparison set that helps people understand it |
| What can be proved? | Results, process, expertise, behaviour, credentials, or evidence |
| What will the brand refuse to be? | The boundaries that create a sharper position |

Internal positioning statement:

`For [best-fit audience] facing [problem or trigger], [brand] is the [category or frame] that [meaningful outcome] because [credible reason to believe]. Unlike [main alternative], it [specific difference].`

Do not publish this mechanically. Use it to force choices, then write public language that sounds human.

## Messaging hierarchy templates

### One-liner

Use seven to twelve words where possible. Name the customer and outcome. Remove technology terms unless the technology itself is the buying reason.

`We help [audience] achieve [outcome] without [pain or tradeoff].`

Test three versions:

- outcome-led
- problem-led
- point-of-view-led

### Elevator pitch

Write three or four sentences:

1. Who it is for and what situation they face.
2. What the brand does and how the experience works.
3. What changes for the customer.
4. Why this brand is credible or different.

### Core message map

| Core message | Why it matters | Proof | Objection it answers | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|---|---|
|  |  |  |  |  |

Keep the list short. If everything is a core message, the hierarchy has failed.

### Proof narrative

Use a specific structure:

`before -> tension or cost -> intervention -> observable result -> why it matters now`

Use numbers, dates, named processes, customer language, or visible product behaviour where available. Label estimates and illustrative examples as such.

## Voice and tone

Choose three or four voice attributes. For each one write:

- what it means in practice
- what it does not mean
- a strong example
- a weak example
- how it changes in a sensitive or urgent context

Example:

| Attribute | It is | It is not |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | Clear, concrete, and willing to name the tradeoff | Rude, abrupt, or oversimplified |
| Warm | Human, attentive, and respectful | Cutesy, vague, or overly familiar |
| Confident | Specific and accountable | Loud, arrogant, or absolute without proof |

Voice stays stable. Tone can become calmer in support, more focused in a proposal, more playful in social content, and more careful in an incident message.

## Messaging QA

Before publishing, ask:

- Can a customer repeat the point after one reading?
- Does the first sentence say why it matters?
- Does every strong claim have evidence or a clear qualifier?
- Is the language specific enough to exclude the wrong audience?
- Does the copy sound like a person, not a category page?
- Does it work aloud, on mobile, and for a non-expert reader?
- Are the words translated or localised by someone who understands the target market?
