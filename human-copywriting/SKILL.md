# Human Copywriting

Use this skill for the reader, argument and offer structure. When writing as Papa Kojo, follow [references/voice-profile.md](references/voice-profile.md); an explicitly requested voice or brand takes priority. For a simple repair, use only the relevant editing steps. Never force persuasion into condolences, apologies or routine updates.

## Core standard

Writing should sound like a person who has noticed something, thought about it, and has a reason for telling this reader now.

Prioritize these principles in this order:

1. Reader fit: identify one reader, their situation, their problem, and the change the piece should create.
2. Clear thought: reduce the idea to one controlling point before trying to make it stylish.
3. Specificity: use concrete details, examples, scenes, decisions, numbers, or consequences. Replace abstract claims with evidence from life or the brief.
4. Visible perspective: include what the writer saw, did, believes, learned, noticed, feared, chose, or would do differently. Never invent lived experience, proof, or a personal anecdote.
5. Structure: make the movement of the piece obvious. Use one idea per paragraph and let each paragraph earn the next.
6. Natural language: prefer familiar words, concrete verbs, varied sentence lengths, and clean paragraphs. Cut jargon, inflated adjectives, empty transitions, and generic conclusions.
7. Ethical persuasion: make the value and next action clear without false urgency, fabricated authority, emotional manipulation, or unsupported promises.

Repeated ideas deserve refinement, not forced novelty. A strong body of work returns to a few important ideas from new angles, examples, and experiences.

## Modes

Choose the mode that matches the request:

- Write: ask only for missing information that materially changes the result. Infer routine context; never assume personal facts or proof.
- Rewrite: preserve the facts, intent, and recognizable voice unless the user asks for a new voice. Improve clarity, specificity, rhythm, structure, and reader movement.
- Diagnose: identify the most consequential problems, quote the affected fragments and explain their effect. Do not rewrite unless requested.
- Diagnose and rewrite: provide a brief diagnosis, then finished copy. Follow a different order if explicitly requested.

## Working method

1. Define the job. Extract the audience, context, desired action or feeling, format, length, voice, proof, and constraints.
2. Find the human material. Extract the concrete situation, observed detail, personal angle, conflict, example, or result from supplied evidence. If it is absent, use a clearly marked placeholder rather than generic filler.
3. Choose a simple spine. Usually use one of these: problem to stakes to solution; observation to story to lesson; claim to example to implication; promise to proof to action; or question to answer to next step.
4. Draft for meaning. Put the strongest relevant idea early. Keep paragraphs focused. Use a hook that creates legitimate curiosity, names a real problem, offers a concrete promise, or introduces a useful contrast.
5. Add the proof. Give the reader a reason to believe: a specific example, process, comparison, result, demonstration, credible source, or honest limitation.
6. Perform the human pass. Replace vague language with detail, add the writer's actual perspective, vary rhythm, remove stock phrasing, read it aloud, and keep a little natural texture without manufacturing mistakes.
7. Perform the copy pass. Check relevance, benefits, objections, stakes, sequence, and the next action. Features explain what something is; benefits explain what changes for the reader.
8. Perform the truth pass. Check every factual claim, number, testimonial, quote, and promise. Mark uncertainty instead of smoothing it away.

## Useful structures

Use formulas as scaffolding, never as a substitute for thought. See `references/structures.md` for reusable patterns.

- Micro-story: relatable problem, consequence or tension, decision, useful change.
- Explainer: plain point, enough supporting reasoning, a useful example where needed, practical implication.
- Sales page or email: reader problem, desired outcome, mechanism, proof, objections, offer, action.
- Personal post: specific observation, personal scene, interpretation, and an ending suited to the point.
- Essay: question, thesis, sequence of reasoning, counterpoint or limitation, conclusion that changes the reader's view.

## AI and humanization

Do not treat "humanize this" as a magic instruction. First diagnose the cause of the stiffness. Typical causes are generic claims, missing experience, uniform rhythm, over-explaining, corporate vocabulary, weak verbs, and conclusions that say nothing new.

Use AI as a thinking partner, researcher, structural editor, critic, or question generator. Give it the real audience, source material, point of view, examples, desired effect, and forbidden claims. Do not ask it to fabricate stories, credentials, quotes, testimonials, statistics, or personal history. The final voice should be grounded in the writer's material, not in detector-bypass tricks.

Down-weight advice that defines human writing as random typos, banned punctuation, word swapping, "perplexity," or tricks designed only to beat an AI detector. These may expose symptoms in some drafts, but they do not create insight, credibility, or a real voice.

## Default voice for this user

Unless the brief says otherwise, write like a smart, thoughtful Ghanaian friend: calm, direct, warm, plainspoken, and observant. Explain the why. Use simple English and concrete examples. Avoid corporate jargon, hype, fake certainty, forced sophistication, and em dashes. Preserve intentional expression while correcting dictation errors. Override this default when the requested audience, format, or brand voice requires it.

## Final quality check

Before returning a draft, check:

- Is the intended reader obvious?
- Is there one clear main idea?
- Does the opening create a real reason to continue?
- Is there enough concrete detail for this task, without invented filler?
- Can the reader see the writer's perspective?
- Are paragraphs and sentences easy to follow aloud?
- Does every claim have support or honest uncertainty?
- Is the benefit or implication clear?
- If an action is needed, is it proportionate and specific?
- Did the edit remove stiffness without flattening personality?
