# Human copywriting structures and diagnostics

These are starting shapes. Replace the placeholders with real material and change the order when the reader's needs demand it.

## 1. Problem to change

Use for short persuasive posts, landing-page sections, and practical advice.

1. Name the reader's specific situation or frustration.
2. Show the cost, risk, or emotional tension without exaggerating it.
3. Explain the underlying mistake or useful reframe.
4. Give a small, concrete next step.
5. Close with the likely change, proof, or an honest limitation.

Example skeleton:

> [Specific situation] creates [observable difficulty]. [Supported explanation] helps explain why. [Practical action] may help because [reason or evidence].

## 2. Personal observation to lesson

Use for a human-sounding personal post or essay opening.

1. Start with a scene, decision, message, mistake, or surprising observation.
2. Say what you initially believed or expected.
3. Show what changed your mind.
4. Extract one lesson, not a list of generic morals.
5. Give the reader a way to test the lesson.

Keep the scene true. If the writer has not provided a scene, ask for one or label a placeholder.

## 3. Claim to proof

Use for explainers, educational content, and thought leadership.

1. State the claim in one plain sentence.
2. Give the mechanism or reasoning.
3. Show one example, comparison, process, or result.
4. Mention where the claim does not apply.
5. State the practical implication.

This is stronger than stacking unsupported opinions or motivational lines.

## 4. Reader movement for copy

Use for ads, sales pages, email, and calls to action.

Attention: lead with a relevant problem, desire, contrast, question, or specific promise.

Relevance: show that you understand the reader's situation in their language.

Value: explain the mechanism and outcome. Translate features into benefits.

Proof: use demonstrations, details, credible evidence, examples, or transparent limits.

Objections: answer the strongest reasonable doubt before asking for action.

Action: state one next step and what happens after it.

## 5. Essay spine

Question or tension -> thesis -> ordered reasoning -> example or scene -> counterpoint or limitation -> implication -> conclusion.

The conclusion should not merely repeat the introduction. It should leave the reader with a sharper view, a decision, or a useful question.

## 6. Six-question pre-draft

Answer these before writing sales or promotional copy:

- What is the promise?
- What exact problem does the reader have?
- Whose problem is it, in what situation?
- What changes if the reader accepts the offer or idea?
- What should the reader feel, understand, or believe by the end?
- What is the smallest clear next action?

## 7. Diagnosis rubric

Use the relevant questions to identify concrete weaknesses. Scores are optional editorial judgments, not measurements. Skip dimensions that do not fit the task:

| Dimension | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Reader | Could one real person recognize their situation? |
| Idea | Can the main point be said in one sentence? |
| Specificity | Are there concrete details, examples, or consequences? |
| Perspective | Could this have been written by anyone, or is the writer visible? |
| Structure | Does each paragraph advance the thought? |
| Rhythm | Does it sound natural when read aloud? |
| Proof | Is there a reason to believe the claim? |
| Action | Is the desired next step clear and proportionate? |
| Truth | Are claims, promises, and examples supported? |

## 8. The human pass

Search for:

- generic openings such as broad claims about "the modern world"
- abstract nouns where a concrete verb would do
- repeated sentence shapes
- paragraphs that make more than one point
- empty transitions such as "in conclusion" or "moreover"
- inflated adjectives and corporate phrases
- advice with no example or consequence
- a personal story that is decorative rather than relevant
- a conclusion that only restates the promise

Then ask:

- What did the writer actually see or do?
- What detail would make this believable?
- What would the reader ask next?
- Which sentence sounds like a brochure rather than a person?
- Where can one sentence be cut without losing meaning?

Do not add typos or awkwardness merely to appear human. Natural writing is clear, specific, and alive, not randomly damaged.
