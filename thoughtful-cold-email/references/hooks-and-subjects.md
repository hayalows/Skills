# Hooks and subjects: practical guide

Source: The Cold Email Manifesto, chapter 6; chapter 10 adapts outreach to links, podcast guests and career. The book proposes a subject, customized first line, case study, call to action and signature. Its sample leads with a specific client project and gives the sender’s expertise afterward. It favors a short sincere compliment grounded in an identifiable detail; its claims that one formula or subject always works are unverified.

After the greeting, consider at least three honest, distinct opening options: (1) a specific project or published idea and why it matters for this request; (2) a relevant shared challenge stated as an observation, not an unsupported diagnosis; (3) a concrete resource or contribution suited to the recipient; (4) a real, relevant connection only if verified. Prefer one sentence, with an explicit bridge into why you are writing. Do not manufacture enthusiasm, prior familiarity, mutual acquaintances, experience, statistics or specific results. Avoid overused ‘I came across your work’ as empty filler; cite a meaningful detail. No invented facts. Avoid making all emails identical.

Identity still matters: integrate it naturally after the hook, for example ‘At [organization], I’m working on [specific work]’, and give name and role in the signature. If a shared inbox may not reach the named person, address the team or politely request forwarding.

The book reports success with ‘Quick Question’ and variants, a recipient name, interview invite, story pitch, and actual introduction. Treat as anecdotes, not proven winners. Prefer a brief truthful subject tied to recipient, resource or action; ‘Quick question’ may be tested if appropriate but is vague and not a default. Never imply a referral, existing relationship, urgency or interview invitation that does not exist. Compare two credible subjects based on clarity, fit and curiosity, not manipulation.

Match case study and proof to purpose. Where no outcome evidence exists, use a real work sample or resource and a modest, concrete proposition. One easy-to-answer request should fit the message: a brief question, invitation to review one item, or permission to discuss an idea. No guarantee any approach will earn a response.

Self-check: Could this opener be sent unchanged to ten unrelated people? Does the first sentence earn the next? Is the named detail verifiable and relevant? Does the message identify the sender naturally? Does the request cost the recipient a reasonable amount of effort?
