# Thoughtful Cold Email

Help the user earn a relevant response through clear, truthful, recipient-centered outreach. Adapt the depth to the task: a single email needs a short focused pass; campaign strategy needs research, operating controls, measurement, and testing. Do not assume every outreach is a sales pitch.

## Workflow

1. Identify the sender, audience, relationship, objective, desired next step, evidence, constraints, and context. Infer reasonable details; ask one focused question only if a missing fact would materially change the draft. Never invent a client's needs, results, role, relationship, credentials, or praise. Label placeholders clearly.
2. Check recipient fit and the right contact. For named people or organizations, research current reliable sources when useful and permitted; distinguish sourced observation, inference, and unknown. Cite sources outside the email when helpful. Do not claim to have read or admired something without evidence. Avoid intrusive personal details.
3. Form the proposition: who is it for, what relevant problem or opportunity, what can the sender credibly offer, and why now? If the offer is too broad, offer a concrete, deliverable hypothesis, not a made-up diagnosis or promise. For jobs, partnerships, publications, or research, identify mutual value rather than force a sales case study.
4. Select the strongest relevant evidence: a verifiable result, work sample, method, specific experience, or honest proposed first step. If proof is absent, say what can actually be done and use a placeholder or request evidence. Never fabricate social proof or numbers.
5. Put a specific recipient-relevant observation, shared problem, or useful idea directly after the greeting. Avoid defaulting to “My name is” or generic pleasantries. Place sender identity naturally in a later sentence and signature. Generate several distinct opening candidates privately, choose the strongest truthful one, and connect it to the purpose; use a compliment only if sincere and supported. Read references/hooks-and-subjects.md for the book’s methods and practical options. Write a clear subject reflecting the message; use a natural greeting; lead with a relevant observation or reason for contact, connect it to the offer and evidence, then make one low-friction, proportionate request. A compliment is optional. Prefer a real observation over generic flattery. Keep it easy to read; length follows the task, not a rigid word count. Use the user's voice when writing on their behalf, and any requested brand voice where appropriate.
6. Output the requested deliverable first. If useful, provide a short rationale, fact-check flags, and one alternative subject; do not overwhelm a simple draft. For an audit, diagnose specifically before rewriting if requested. Do not assume permission to send: drafting or research is not permission to contact anyone. Send only on explicit instructions and use recipient-resolution guidance if applicable.
7. For a sequence, propose a small number of spaced, relevant follow-ups, each adding a useful detail or making the next step easier. Stop on a reply, refusal, opt-out, or hard bounce; honor suppression across lists and channels. Do not imply consent, guilt the reader, or manufacture urgency. Respect user preference and applicable law.
8. For campaign help, read references/campaigns.md. For adaptation and examples, read references/patterns.md. For source analysis and cautions, read references/book-analysis.md.

## Quality gate

Check: Is this the correct person? Is the reason for contacting them relevant and supported? Is the benefit specific and deliverable? Is each claim true and the sender identifiable? Can the recipient understand the request in one read? Does the tone sound human? Is there one proportionate next step? Is there a respectful way to decline where appropriate? Would the message still be worthwhile if read without the compliment? Remove manipulation, fake familiarity, unsupported metrics, irrelevant details, and pressure. No guaranteed outcomes.
