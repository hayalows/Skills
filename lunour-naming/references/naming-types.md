# Naming territories

Use this reference when choosing creative routes or explaining why a candidate belongs in a territory. These are working categories, not rigid boxes. A strong name can sit in two categories at once.

## 1. Evocative or emotional

Names that suggest a feeling, atmosphere, or state rather than the literal offer.

Use when the brand needs to own a human benefit such as calm, freedom, speed, care, or confidence.

Test whether the name creates a feeling before the explanation is supplied.

## 2. Invented or coined

Made-up words, transformed spellings, or meaningful roots assembled into a new word.

Use when distinctiveness, international flexibility, and trademark potential matter.

Guard against awkward consonant clusters, fake sophistication, and names that need constant spelling correction.

## 3. Metaphor or analogy

A name borrowed from another domain, such as nature, movement, light, tools, places, or animals.

Use when the company has a large or abstract ambition and needs a narrative container.

Prefer metaphors with several useful associations. Do not explain every layer until the name feels forced.

## 4. Portmanteau or mashup

Two meaningful words or roots compressed into a new concept.

Use when two ideas genuinely belong together and neither word is enough by itself.

Check the seam, stress pattern, spelling, and whether the result sounds like an accidental word.

## 5. Experiential or verb-friendly

Names that can become a natural action, noun, or phrase in everyday language.

Use for products used frequently or shared socially. Test sentences such as "send it through ___" or "we ___ed it."

## 6. Descriptive or functional

Names that explain what the product does.

Use when immediate clarity matters more than distinctiveness, such as a narrowly scoped utility or early test product.

Expect weaker ownability and more category competition. Add personality through the identity system.

## 7. Founder or eponymous

A founder's name or a person-like name used as the brand.

Use when personal reputation, craft, or authority is part of the value and the founder is comfortable with long-term association.

Avoid it when the company must be easily transferred, broaden beyond the founder, or feel independent of one person.

## 8. Place or geographic

A real or imagined location used for the associations it carries.

Use when the place expresses scale, origin, craft, or a point of view. Research whether the reference is respectful, pronounceable, and legally usable.

## 9. Abstract sound design

A name with little inherent meaning, engineered for sound, rhythm, and visual shape.

Use when the company can invest in building meaning over time. It needs a strong story, repeated use, and disciplined identity work.

## 10. Number or symbol

Numbers, initials, punctuation, or symbols are central to the name.

Use sparingly. Check how people say it aloud, type it, search it, and distinguish it from an account number or model code.

## Territory selection matrix

| Desired feeling or advantage | Territories to test |
|---|---|
| Bold and scaled | Metaphor, animal, invented, place |
| Warm and human | Evocative, experiential, eponymous |
| Clear and accessible | Descriptive, portmanteau, verb-friendly |
| Technical and modern | Invented, abstract, number or symbol |
| Premium and established | Eponymous, place, evocative |
| Memetic or habit-forming | Experiential, verb-friendly, metaphor |

## Sound prompts

These are starting hypotheses, not laws. Hard consonants can feel crisp or forceful. Softer consonants can feel gentle or fluid. Open vowels often travel well across languages. The whole word, stress pattern, and listener's language matter more than one letter.

Always say the name aloud at normal speed, in a sentence, over a phone call, and beside the names of its parent and sibling brands.
