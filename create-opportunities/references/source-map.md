## Source editions and reading scope

This skill was developed from the user-supplied PDFs of Jay Yang's **You Can Just Do Things: The Power of Permissionless Action** (copyright 2025, 124 PDF pages) and **Work With The Best: How to Get the Life You Want by Helping Those Who Have It** (copyright 2026, 256 PDF pages). Page numbers below are PDF page positions, including front matter, not inferred print pagination. Other editions may differ.

The reading covered both books' main text, exercises, examples, conclusions, and end matter. Image-based content was visually inspected, including the opportunity notes, second-brain examples, pitch-deck montage, Contact Quadrant, outreach examples, and public-pitch examples. QR-linked bonuses were not opened; the skill does not depend on their contents.

This is an original practical synthesis. It does not reproduce the books or claim endorsement by Jay Yang. Historical anecdotes illustrate the author's argument, not causal proof that a tactic will produce the same outcome. You Can Just Do Things acknowledges dramatized or paraphrased minor details in its bibliography note on PDF p. 120. Embedded quotations and factual claims have not been independently verified for this skill.

## Named methods and attribution

- **TAG:** Target, Audit, Gift. Both books credit Charlie Hoehn. This skill adds evidence ledgers, effort caps, rights checks, and quality gates.
- **Four pillars:** Learning, Earning, Network, Story, from Work With The Best chapter 8. The author allows different categories and weights. This skill adds confidence labels, scoring anchors, sensitivity checks, and hard-limit gates.
- **Contact Quadrant:** Known/unknown people crossed with private/public delivery, shown on PDF p. 152 of Work With The Best. Referrals, cold outreach, content, and public pitches are its four routes. The classification simplifies audiences; real content can reach both known and unknown people.
- **AIDA:** Attention, Interest, Desire, Action, applied in Work With The Best chapter 12. Treat it as a useful communication structure. This skill does not independently validate the book's historical attribution or date.
- **3 P's:** Personalize, Prove, Plan, PDF p. 178 of Work With The Best.
- **A.W.A.R.E.:** Ask, Who you are, Achievements, Reason, Extra work, PDF pp. 230–234 of Work With The Best.
- **T3B3:** Top three and bottom three review, attributed to Noah Kagan on PDF p. 85 of Work With The Best.
- **Obsession Arc:** Focused skill-building period, Work With The Best pp. 86–90. This skill adapts intensity to obligations and recovery rather than prescribing the author's hours.
- **Chain reading and second brain:** Work With The Best chapter 4; related influence study also appears in You Can Just Do Things chapter 4.
- **What, Why, How / But, Therefore:** Educational and storytelling structures discussed in Work With The Best pp. 208–210. Use only where the actual content supports them.
- **Follow-up sequence:** First follow-up 3 days after original contact, second 7 days after that, third 7 days later; stop after three unanswered follow-ups, Work With The Best pp. 184–186.

## Original additions and limits

The six-step opportunity loop, routing instructions, worksheets, score anchors, default sample cap, quality rubric, measurement ledger, scoped engagement brief, and hypothetical examples were created for this skill. They operationalize ideas but are not quotations or author-prescribed systems.

Important tensions were resolved deliberately:

1. Preparation versus action: prepare enough to make a useful bounded attempt; stop researching when the next uncertainty can be tested through work.
2. Intensity versus anti-goals: focus must fit the person's obligations and limits.
3. Learning versus earning: learning value cannot erase immediate income needs.
4. Persistence versus sunk costs: repeat when evidence supports it; change or stop when it does not.
5. Public proof versus privacy: share original work responsibly, with rights and unofficial status clear.
6. Giving versus exploitation: a gift has no obligation; requested ongoing work needs terms.
7. Opportunity stories versus probabilities: no invented odds, guaranteed replies, or claims that effort always wins.

Do not carry forward the books' broad claims as universal facts, including near-guaranteed public-pitch attention, “no downside” claims, assumptions about silence, unverified psychological effects, salary examples, or historical statistics. The books are useful sources of ideas; outcomes require actual testing in context.
