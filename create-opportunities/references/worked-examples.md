# Worked examples

All organizations, figures, and situations below are hypothetical. Use the decision patterns, not the invented facts. Do not treat these as the user's history.

## A. A designer in Ghana seeking hospitality clients

**Request:** “I design flyers, have three spare hours this week, and want to find a local hotel client without spending money.”

**Direction:** Preserve existing income and obligations. Seek evidence that one hotel's public guest-information flow could benefit from clearer communication. The first result is a useful conversation, not a promised contract.

**Audit:** In this hypothetical example, the public page lists two room types but provides no visible explanation of what differs. That is an observation. Whether guests abandon inquiries is unknown.

**Gift:** Create one original comparison graphic using verified room information. Where prices or amenities are unavailable, mark them for confirmation instead of inventing them. Include a mobile-readable version and a short note explaining the proposed clarity improvement.

**Cap:** Up to two hours to research and draft, one hour to review and prepare the message. No paid tools and no copied licensed hotel photography.

**Draft:**

“Hi [Name], I noticed your page shows the two room types, but the differences aren't clear at a glance. I made a simple comparison layout using the details currently listed. A few items need your confirmation, so I've marked them in the draft.

Would this be useful for answering room inquiries? If it is, I can put together a small paid set of guest-information designs.”

**Review:** Measure whether the hotel confirms a need and wants to discuss scope. Do not claim the design increases bookings before testing. A reply saying “nice design” is encouraging but not a confirmed project.

**Transferable lesson:** Start with a guest task, make a useful improvement, and offer a bounded next step.

## B. A student with no client results

**Request:** “I want a marketing internship but have only class projects. Help me pitch a startup.”

**Choice:** A relevant team with an observable onboarding problem is more useful than a famous company selected for status. Keep required application steps.

**Hypothetical observation:** The public product description assumes familiarity with an industry term. New users may not understand the benefit, but conversion impact is unknown.

**Gift:** Rewrite the introduction for a first-time visitor, explain the audience assumption, and draft a three-message onboarding sequence. Call it an unofficial work sample. Use no fictitious testimonials or metrics.

**Draft:**

“Hi [Name], I'm studying marketing and have been learning about onboarding. I made a short sample for [Product]: a clearer introductory section and three onboarding messages based on your public product information.

I haven't tested them with users yet. Would you be open to a quick look at whether I've understood the audience? I'm also interested in an internship where I could help with this work and get feedback.”

**Review:** Specific feedback can be the first success signal. Do not hide the lack of commercial experience or claim the sample has already improved activation.

## C. A job offer that scores well but fails a hard constraint

**Request:** “I have an offer with strong learning, but less pay. Should I accept?”

**Hypothetical facts:** Current role pays GHS 4,500 net monthly. New role pays GHS 2,800 after required work expenses. Essential obligations total GHS 3,600. There is no savings buffer. The new employer's mentoring schedule is also unconfirmed.

**Result:** The offer leaves a monthly shortfall of GHS 800. Do not recommend accepting unchanged because Learning or Story is high. Ask for verified mentoring arrangements and negotiate compensation, part-time structure, or a later start. Include staying in the current role with a small learning project as an option.

**Comparison:** Learning for the new role remains unknown until actual duties and feedback access are confirmed. A claim that “you will learn a lot” does not earn an 8/10.

**Transferable lesson:** Hard constraints precede scores. Financial needs are part of the decision, not an objection to dismiss.

## D. A builder with public app analytics

**Request:** “Help me use my app's all-time analytics to approach an organization for work.”

**Inputs required:** Verified period, metric definitions, event counts, unique-visitor method, geographic breakdown if lawful, and actual usage evidence. Read supplied or authorized data before writing the case study.

**Hypothetical data:** 10,000 page views, 2,000 unique visitors, 600 search events. Those are different measures. Search events are not necessarily 600 people; unique visitors are not registered users. Country counts do not prove local business demand.

**Gift:** Produce a one-page case study: user problem, what was built, verified usage, limits, and one relevant application for the prospective organization. Include a sample workflow tailored to its public need.

**Draft excerpt:** “The app recorded [verified count and definition] over [period]. I built it to help people [specific task]. I've put together a small example of how a similar approach could support [verified organizational need].”

**Review:** The organization's interest in the actual work matters more than a large headline metric. Do not infer employment readiness or revenue from traffic alone.

## E. Silence after a good sample

**Request:** “I sent a sample and got no reply. Should I tag the founder and all their friends?”

**Response:** Treat silence as unknown. Check recipient relevance, whether the contact route was appropriate, artifact access, and whether the ask was clear. Prepare a small value-adding follow-up if it fits the timing and no refusal exists. Keep the cap. Do not coordinate public pressure or contact unrelated friends.

**Alternative:** An original public case study can demonstrate skill to a wider audience if the material is safe to publish. It should focus on useful thinking, label unofficial work, and avoid turning nonresponse into a public grievance.

**Stop rule:** After the agreed bounded sequence, move on. Save the learning and choose another qualified target.

## F. Curiosity without direction

**Request:** “I keep reading about finance, design, and coding. I don't know what to commit to.”

**Action:** Choose one two-hour experiment connecting an existing strength to a real task. For example, create a clear explanation of a public dataset, with a small chart and source notes. This is a hypothetical task, not a claim of expertise.

**Review separately:** Was the work enjoyable? Was the explanation accurate? Did feedback show improvement? Is there a beneficiary? What skill caused friction? A single experiment cannot establish a permanent calling, but it can identify the next useful attempt.

**Next step:** Practice the bottleneck for a month before adding another major commitment. Keep a simple record rather than a complex self-improvement system.
