# Direction and decisions

Use this reference to identify a fitting direction, design an experiment, or compare consequential opportunities. These worksheets are original operational adaptations of the books.

## Define a working direction

Start with a desired ordinary week. Capture the work the person wants to do, people they want near them, location and flexibility, energy, responsibilities, and what money needs to support. Avoid filling in family aspirations, beliefs, or status goals for them.

Turn that description into a decision rule. For example: “Prefer work that gives me regular practice in customer research while preserving my evening responsibilities and covering essential expenses.” Make it specific enough to filter an offer and flexible enough to revise.

If direction is unclear, ask for evidence rather than a permanent life purpose:

- What do others already ask them to help with?
- Which tasks do they learn relatively quickly, and what shows that?
- Which activities hold interest after novelty wears off?
- What kind of work do they admire, and have they tried its unglamorous parts?
- What would they willingly practice for the next month?

Treat apparent strengths as hypotheses. Grades, interest, or compliments are clues, not proof of a profitable advantage. Identify complementary skills solving the same beneficiary's problem. Design plus customer understanding plus clear writing can support one service; unrelated interests may not.

## Define anti-goals and the financial floor

Write two or three non-negotiables in observable terms. “Balance” is vague; “No routine calls after 8 p.m. because I provide care then” is assessable. Separate hard limits from negotiable preferences.

Calculate only with supplied or explicitly hypothetical numbers:

`Monthly financial floor = essential personal costs + dependent obligations + recurring work costs + required debt payments`

`Estimated runway in months = liquid funds available for this purpose / expected monthly shortfall`

Label omissions such as taxes, insurance, variable income, or currency conversion. Do not assume savings or family support. Use a range when inputs fluctuate. Do not invent a universal runway rule or advise investment/liquidation; use appropriate financial guidance for those questions.

Ask whether the schedule, income uncertainty, and unpaid effort leave enough capacity to learn and deliver, not only whether the person can survive this month.

## Classify the experiment

| Decision type | Example | Useful response |
|---|---|---|
| Easy to reverse, like trying on a hat | One work sample, lesson, informational conversation | Act within a modest cap and review |
| Reversible with cost, like a haircut | Short paid trial, course enrollment, reducing hours | Define costs, terms, exit, review date |
| Hard to reverse, like a tattoo | Leaving a degree, relocating, taking on debt | Gather evidence, compare options, write a memo |

These are metaphors used in Work With The Best, not objective classifications. Assess circumstances. A small fee can be a large commitment for someone with little spare money.

## Compare opportunities with four pillars

The book scores Learning, Earning, Network, and Story out of ten. Start there, then adapt to the user's goals. Apply hard limits before arithmetic.

| Pillar | Ask | Evidence | Common error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Learning | What skill will I practice and who reviews it? | Actual duties, supervisor, feedback cadence, project access | Giving a famous company high marks for an unrelated role |
| Earning | What is dependable net income after costs? | Written pay terms, payment reliability, hours, expenses | Counting future earning potential as cash available now |
| Network | Who will I genuinely collaborate with? | Team structure, interactions, mentorship access | Assuming a logo grants access to the founder |
| Story | What credible work can I demonstrate afterward? | Deliverables, ownership, reference/portfolio rights | Treating proximity to prestige as accomplishment |

Use score anchors consistently: 0 means no benefit or unacceptable cost; 3 means limited benefit; 5 means plausible benefit with uncertainty; 8 means strong benefit with concrete evidence; 10 means exceptional fit with unusually strong evidence. These anchors are this skill's addition.

For each score, record evidence and confidence separately. Use “unknown” when information is absent rather than a fabricated middle score. If a total requires an estimate, label the assumption and show a plausible range.

For weighted comparisons, choose weights explicitly and normalize their sum to one:

`Weighted score = sum(weight × pillar score)`

The result is on a 0–10 scale. An equally weighted unweighted sum is out of 40. Do not confuse the scales. Include the current path and a lower-risk modified offer. A pay cut requires a covered financial floor and concrete learning, not a vague promise.

Run a sensitivity check: would changing one uncertain score by two points reverse the choice? If yes, gather that missing fact or test before commitment. Do not let a small numeric difference hide a major personal tradeoff.

## Write a decision memo

State the decision, outcome, current baseline, and deadline. Present options with evidence and tradeoffs. Record non-negotiables, financial impact, actual daily tasks, and unknowns. Explain the strongest case against the favored option. Specify what would change the recommendation and whether a trial, deferral, or negotiated adjustment is available.

End with a recommendation proportionate to evidence: choose, reject, negotiate, or investigate a named fact. Make reasoning inspectable instead of dressing intuition in numbers.

## Design a clarity experiment

Pick one activity the person can attempt. Define a question: “Do I enjoy the work and improve with feedback?” Produce a small artifact. Set time and money caps. Identify a reviewer or comparison standard. Review enjoyment, progress, demand, and constraints separately.

Prefer sequential experiments to simultaneous commitments. If income is urgent, preserve an income path while experimenting. Continuing a useful job or formal education can be deliberate, not evidence of low ambition.
