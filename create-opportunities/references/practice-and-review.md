# Practice, delivery, and review

Build capability, keep promises after a positive reply, and improve from results.

## Build a skill ladder

Identify intended contribution and current bottleneck. Choose one complementary skill increasing usefulness to the same audience. Connect it to a deliverable, not only a course or certificate.

Map isolated tasks toward complete outcomes. For a designer: visual assets, then campaign clarity, then a coherent inquiry flow with measurement. Do not assume each stage automatically increases pay; demand, access, negotiation, and execution matter.

Use the books' learning methods:

- Study primary work and identify mechanisms, not only surface style.
- Follow influences through chain reading; test one practice before consuming another pile of material.
- Build real projects; learn what the current problem requires.
- Teach genuinely understood material with attribution and honest limits.
- Review work and request specific feedback.
- Use small peer groups with consent, purpose, and brief structured exchanges.

Keep a knowledge record: source, idea, relevance, application, result. A plain document is enough. Do not build an elaborate database before the habit exists or save unlicensed full texts as notes.

## Run a 30-day practice experiment

The book proposes trying one skill intensely for 30 days. Adapt intensity to responsibilities. Treat an Obsession Arc as bounded focused practice with recovery and a review date.

| Period | Work | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Start | Skill, audience, baseline, schedule, constraints | Initial artifact and rubric |
| Week 1 | Study examples; complete small repetitions | Samples and observations |
| Week 2 | Build one real task end to end | Artifact and feedback |
| Week 3 | Improve the weak component | Before/after with limits |
| Week 4 | Complete a fresh task | New artifact and review |

Spend most practice time producing and revising, with some study. No ratio is mandatory. Four 30-minute weekly sessions can be a valid starting commitment.

Define quality criteria before comparing. For copy: audience clarity, truth, logical sequence, next step. For design: task completion, hierarchy, readability, accessibility. For research: sources, calculations, uncertainty. Do not use likes as the only measure.

At day 30, evaluate interest in actual work, improvement, useful contribution, demand, and fit with obligations. Continue, change practice, try another skill, or retain it for noncommercial use.

## Ask for feedback and close the loop

Show the attempt, identify weakness, and ask something quickly answerable. Replace “How do I become good?” with “Here are two versions; is the offer clear to a first-time reader?”

Record suggestion, change, and result. Report outcomes honestly when appropriate and authorized. Qualitative observations are valid; never invent conversion gains.

Keep responsibility for judgment. A mentor's suggestion is information, not an order. Test against evidence and constraints. Do not require obedience to every suggestion as proof of seriousness.

## Turn interest into a scoped engagement

Confirm whether the sample addresses a real priority. Ask what differs internally from public observations. Correct scope rather than defending an audit at all costs.

Prepare a brief covering:

- Outcome and beneficiary.
- Deliverables and exclusions.
- Access, assets, permissions.
- Decision and approval owner.
- Timeline, reviews, feedback process.
- Success measures and available baseline.
- Compensation, payment timing, expenses, revision limits.
- Usage, portfolio, and reference rights.
- End date and continuation conditions.

A reply is not a contract or permission to edit live systems. Self-directed samples can be free; ongoing work, requested production tasks, and extended trials need terms. Use relevant legal guidance when legality is at issue.

Choose an “extra ounce” within scope: clearer handoff, accessibility check, edge-case note, or concise next steps. Do not substitute scope creep, purchases, or unauthorized decisions for initiative.

## Measure the opportunity loop

Use a small ledger unless a CRM is needed. Record target, fit, artifact, effort, route, date if actually sent, response, next action, review date. Retain only necessary authorized private contact data.

| Stage | Useful signal | Do not confuse with |
|---|---|---|
| Research | Qualified need supported by evidence | Tabs opened |
| Proof | Relevant artifact passing review | Hours or slide count |
| Contact | Relevant reply or introduction | Opens, impressions, likes alone |
| Conversation | Confirmed priority and feasible next step | Friendly praise |
| Trial | Agreed scope and delivery | Endless unpaid tasks |
| Outcome | Paid work, reference, real learning, reusable artifact | Projected impact |

State denominators. If 2 of 5 qualified messages get replies, say 2/5 and note the small sample. Do not claim a reliable 40% response rate.

## Review T3B3 and the bottleneck

Use the books' Top Three / Bottom Three review: inspect the best and weakest work or decisions, explain differences, choose one change. With fewer than six items, review what exists instead of fabricating a full set.

Diagnose locally:

- No credible need: improve selection or research.
- Need, weak sample: practice or reduce scope.
- Good sample, no attention: assess recipient, timing, route.
- Replies, no next step: improve ask or qualify need.
- Trials, no paid continuation: assess value, terms, budget, fit.
- Good results, unsustainable cost: reduce scope, strengthen boundaries, change offer.

Silence alone cannot distinguish diagnoses. State plausible explanations and choose a cheap test.

Continue when relevant evidence or acceptable value appears. Pivot when the target, offer, or route is weak while the goal remains useful. Stop when boundaries fail, costs exceed caps, no meaningful learning appears, demand is absent, or better opportunities exist. Persistence is a decision, not a moral obligation.
