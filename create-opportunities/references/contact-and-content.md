# Contact and content

Select a route fitting the relationship, evidence, and privacy needs. Communicate contribution without pressure.

## Choose the route

| Route | Best fit | Preparation | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Referral | Genuine connection to the right person | Forwardable blurb and sample | Borrowed trust must be respected |
| Cold outreach | Relevant person uses a public contact channel | Observation, proof, one ask | Attention is scarce; silence ambiguous |
| Content | Repeated work demonstrates qualities | Honest body of useful work | Usually slower than direct contact |
| Public pitch | Work is safe to share publicly | Clear ask and original proof | Public attention can create costs |

The books include dramatic outreach. Use relevance and reviewability, without imitating expensive gifts, surprising packages, trespass, coordinated tags, or uncomfortable attention tricks.

## Referrals

Confirm the connection knows the target well enough. Supply the fit, sample link, and forwardable blurb. Make declining easy. Seek double opt-in introductions instead of sharing private contact details without consent.

Do not write unverified praise in the introducer's voice. “They prepared a sample addressing X” is safer than “They are the best person I know for this.” Respect borrowed trust: arrive prepared, keep promises, and provide a factual update when appropriate and authorized.

## Cold outreach

Choose the type deliberately:

- **Appreciation:** specific sincere thanks without a concealed request.
- **Small feedback request:** real attempt and one answerable question.
- **Useful observation:** tactful evidenced improvement and sample.
- **Direct proposal:** contribution, proof, and next step.

Do not mandate compliment-first sequences. A direct respectful proposal can fit. Do not use praise to create obligation.

Use AIDA as a check:

| Part | Purpose | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| Attention | Make relevance apparent before opening | Short accurate subject |
| Interest | Show why it concerns them | Current observation or relevant context |
| Desire | Make contribution assessable | Sample or verified result |
| Action | Reduce ambiguity | One low-friction question |

Keep initial messages usually around 80–150 words, adapted to medium and context. This is a practical default, not an author rule. Use short connected paragraphs and the user's voice. Do not invent experience, enthusiasm, quotations, numbers, or urgency. Avoid subjects pretending to be a reply, calendar invitation, or prior relationship.

Apply the 3 P's from Work With The Best:

1. **Personalize:** could it be sent unchanged to someone else?
2. **Prove:** is visible evidence of ability or useful work included?
3. **Plan:** is the next step clear if they respond positively?

If impressive metrics are absent, use explained work samples. “I built this draft using public product information” is legitimate. Do not imply past client relationships or business impact.

## Follow-ups

The book suggests three follow-ups: day 3, then 7 days later, then another 7 days later. Treat this as an optional ceiling. Adapt to preferences, relationship, and capacity.

Add a new observation, improved sample, or relevant development. Make each message understandable on its own. Keep the effort cap; do not create a large unpaid deliverable for every follow-up. A reminder alone does not meet this standard. If no useful new detail is available, skip the follow-up instead of inventing value.

Stop after refusal, opt-out, lack of fit, or the final unanswered attempt. Do not chase across channels to evade silence. Future re-approach needs new relevant context and respect for preferences. Do not send automatically without explicit instructions or authorized automation.

## Public pitch

Use A.W.A.R.E., as named in Work With The Best:

- **Ask:** opportunity sought, early.
- **Who you are:** relevant accurate background.
- **Achievements:** verified results or labeled samples.
- **Reason:** specific fit with their work.
- **Extra work:** useful artifact made in advance.

Assess suitability first. Keep sensitive weaknesses private. Label unofficial redesigns. Avoid implied endorsement, employment, or partnership. Other opportunities may appear, but no response or hiring outcome is guaranteed.

Use truthful tags sparingly. Do not coordinate pressure or fake endorsements. Visible effort does not create an obligation to reply.

## Build a credible body of work

Start with who the user wants to help and what they need to trust. Choose qualities to demonstrate through actual work. Show analytical clarity through sourced calculations, and reliability through honest accounts of delivery under constraints.

Separate reach from reputation. Many impressions do not prove trust from the right people. Track relevant responses, conversations, inquiries, and usefulness alongside reach.

Create content from work:

1. Case study: problem, action, observed result, limits, lesson.
2. Breakdown: artifact, observation, interpretation, improvement.
3. Learning note: attempt, change, useful takeaway.
4. Attributed synthesis: source idea and own application.
5. Reintroduction: relevant background and intended future content.

Use What, Why, How for education when helpful. Connect stories through actual obstacles and responses, following “But, Therefore” without mechanically inserting the words. Do not manufacture tension or dramatize the user's life.

Check the opening for benefit, relevance, credibility, and reading effort. Avoid unsupported numbers or exaggerated promises. Deliver the opening's promise. Use one CTA when needed; omit it when the reader has what they need.

For “posting upwards,” synthesize public ideas with attribution and real application. Do not reproduce paywalled articles, lectures, books, or large sets of clips. Original useful work shows fit better than flattery.

Choose sustainable cadence and substantial artifacts over daily filler. Keep confidential data out of public posts. Confirm portfolio rights before showing client work.
