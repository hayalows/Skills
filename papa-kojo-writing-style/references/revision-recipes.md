# Revision recipes

Use these after the draft exists, in this order. Each pass asks one question. Fix real problems only. A pattern that carries real meaning can stay.

The examples below were written for this file. They are illustrations, not his words. For the research behind these habits see [anti-slop.md](anti-slop.md); for worked before-and-after cases by format see [examples.md](examples.md).

## Pass 1. Truth

Is every name, number, date, role, feeling and event in the brief or the conversation? Did any "I think" become a fact, or any "helped" become "led"? Fix this first. An awkward true sentence beats a smooth invented one.

## Pass 2. Thread

Could two paragraphs swap places without anything breaking? If yes, the thread is weak. Make each paragraph answer the question the one before it raised. Cut any paragraph that restarts the point.

## Pass 3. Habits that sound like a machine

| Habit | How it shows up | What to do instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Contrast flip** | "It is not X. It is Y." "Most people don't X. They Y." "A bigger income gives you room. It does not decide what you do with it." "We don't just manage numbers; we build futures." | Say the true claim directly, then give its reason. "Income sets how much room you have. Habits decide what happens inside that room" is still a flip; try "Habits matter at every income, but they cannot fix an income that is too small." |
| **Soft reversal** | "The money was made. It is just stuck." "It is not about effort. It is about timing." "X only tells you... It does not tell you..." | Explain the mechanism in order: "You pay the supplier before your customers pay you, so the cash runs out first." |
| **Small borrowed experience** | "I had to hunt for the email." "You may know this feeling." "I was so moved." added to a brief that never said so | Keep to what the brief says happened. Report the finding ("several made contact details hard to find"), not an invented scene of how it felt. |
| **Label before the point** | "Here is the thing." "The reason is often simple." "What tracking can do is..." "This matters because..." "The lesson?" | Delete the label and start with the point itself. |
| **Moral at the end** | "And that is what real friendship means." "Small steps lead to big change." "A small thing is a good place to start." after an invitation | Stop at the last useful sentence, or end with a plain next step. |
| **Rule of three for rhythm** | "clear, simple and powerful", three examples every time, three parallel opening scenes ("Rent is due... School fees come up... A relative calls...") | Use the real number of items. Two is fine. One is often best. |
| **Neat maxim** | A tidy quotable line that sums up the post: "If a tool can do the clicking, students can spend that time talking." | Cut it, or replace it with the plain practical point or the next step. One honest plain sentence beats a clever one. |
| **Stacked fragments** | "No guessing. No stress. Just results." | Join them into one sentence. In Chat and Comment his own short lines ("Well deserved. More wins ahead.") are real and fine, but keep to one or two. |
| **Sweeping opener** | "Many people...", "In today's world...", "Have you ever...?" | Open on this reader's actual situation or the fact from the brief. |
| **Puffed importance** | "plays a vital role", "a testament to", "dynamic", "cutting-edge" | Say the plain fact. "It helps owners see their cash" beats "plays a vital role in financial clarity". |
| **Fancy verbs for "is" and "has"** | "serves as", "stands as", "boasts" | Use "is" and "has". |
| **Signposts** | "In conclusion", "Let's explore", "It is worth noting" | Cut them. The next sentence can stand on its own. |
| **Vague authority** | "Experts say", "Studies show" | Name the source from the brief, or drop the claim. |
| **Hook and engagement questions** | "Ready to take control?" "What do you think? Let me know below." | Cut, unless he truly wants answers to that question. |
| **Proof-signposting** | "That's a number I can point to." "This shows the power of..." | Let the fact stand. The reader can see it. |
| **-ing tails** | "..., highlighting the need for clarity." | Cut the tail, or make it a new sentence with a real subject. |
| **Too fast** | Five short commands in a row; every sentence under eight words | Join related ideas with "and", "so" or "because". Give the reason next to the instruction. |
| **Too smooth** | Every paragraph the same length; every sentence the same shape | Let one paragraph be short because its point is short. Let one sentence run longer because it carries a link. |
| **Sales-template copy** | "Still writing orders on scraps of paper?" "Imagine a world where..." | Describe the reader's real problem in plain words and what changes for them. |

## Pass 4. Presence

Read it once more and ask: is there a person here? Look for at least one of these, taken only from the facts:

- his real stance, said plainly ("I think a side business is worth trying if...");
- a concrete detail from the brief (the sound check, Thursday, the 150 portfolios);
- care for the reader's actual situation ("I know rent can't wait");
- an honest limit ("I'm not sure yet").

If none is there, add one from the facts you have. Never invent one.

## Pass 5. Ending

Would he really end it this way? Good endings he uses: a forward wish, a thank-you, a clear ask, a small next step, an honest limit. Weak endings: a recap, a moral, a slogan, a question nobody needs to answer.
