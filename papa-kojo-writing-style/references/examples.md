# Illustrative examples

These examples were written for this skill. They are not historical quotations, actual events, or user-approved writing. Use the decisions they illustrate; do not recycle their sentences as a voice template.

## 1. Explain a project to a colleague

Brief: A volunteer is making a video for an idea competition. The organiser says it is for people with an early business idea, including those who have not started. Explain the audience to the volunteer briefly. Do not invent benefits or entry details.

Overwritten: 'This exciting initiative empowers aspiring entrepreneurs to transform their vision into reality.'

Possible draft:

The competition is for people who have a business idea, even if they haven’t started yet. So the video should speak to someone who has been thinking about an idea but hasn’t done much with it. Keep the message simple enough for them to recognise themselves in it.

Why: Gives the colleague a usable communication brief. Avoids treating them as a prospective applicant or adding unverified prizes and support.

## 2. Ask for a professional clarification

Brief: Two dates in an onboarding email differ. Ask which date to follow. Dates are not supplied.

Possible draft:

Hi [Name],

I noticed that the onboarding email gives two different start dates. Could you confirm which one I should follow so I can plan properly?

Best regards,
Papa Kojo Mensah

Why: Identifies the exact ambiguity and why an answer matters. Does not invent dates, accuse the recipient or add unnecessary apologies.

## 3. Preserve uncertainty in a rewrite

Original: 'I think the form might be confusing some people because they have to enter the same details twice. We could try removing the repeated fields.'

Overreaching edit: 'The form is driving users away. Removing duplicate fields will increase conversions.'

Possible draft:

I think asking for the same details twice may be confusing people. We could remove the repeated fields and see whether the form becomes easier to complete.

Why: Keeps the hypothesis and proposed test. It does not manufacture user loss or a promised conversion gain.

## 4. Make a reflective idea concrete

Brief: Write a short reflection on learning without enough practice. The user has given no autobiographical story.

Possible draft:

You can finish a course and still struggle to do the work on your own.

A lesson usually gives you a tidy problem. Real work asks you to decide what the problem is, which details matter and what to try when the first attempt fails. That takes practice the lesson cannot do for you.

I think it helps to make something small with what you have learnt before moving to the next course. The difficult part is accepting how rough that first attempt may be.

Why: Develops a causal point and names a tension. Treats this as a proposed viewpoint, not a claim that the user has lived a particular incident. Confirm fit to the user's stated stance before using first person in actual work.

## 5. Keep a church application speakable

Brief: A short application paragraph about listening during disagreement. No personal spiritual experience or quotation is supplied.

Possible draft:

Sometimes we begin planning our reply before the other person has finished speaking. We may have a fair point, but we can still miss what is hurting them. This week, we could try listening a little longer, especially when we feel the need to defend ourselves.

Why: Offers an ordinary situation and a proportionate invitation. Does not invent scripture, personal revelation, authority or testimony.

## 6. Let kindness stay simple

Brief: A friend says their mother has died. Write a brief reply.

Possible draft:

I’m so sorry about your mum. I’m thinking of you. You don’t need to reply now.

Why: Gives care without a lesson, an intrusive question or a promise the sender may not be able to keep.

## 7. Diagnose without rewriting

Draft: 'In today’s fast-paced world, I am uniquely positioned to deliver transformative value.'

Possible diagnosis:

- The opening could fit almost any topic, so it delays your point.
- 'Uniquely positioned' claims an advantage without showing it.
- 'Transformative value' does not tell the reader what you can actually help them do.

Why: Obeys diagnosis-only mode. Does not claim to know who authored the text from its wording.

## 8. Add clarity without flattening emotion

Original: 'I’m frustrated because we agreed on Friday and nobody told me the date had changed. I need a clear answer today.'

Possible draft:

I’m frustrated because we agreed on Friday, and I wasn’t told the date had changed. Please confirm the new date today so I know what to plan for.

Why: Keeps the frustration and urgency. Does not replace a legitimate concern with politeness, or add an accusation about intent.
