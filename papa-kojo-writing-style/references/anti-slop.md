# Anti-slop: what makes writing read as AI, and what to do instead

Researched 2026-11-16. Main sources: Wikipedia's editor guide "Signs of AI writing" (the most detailed public catalogue, built from thousands of real cases), Harry Dry's three rules and conversational-copy guide (marketingexamples.com), Alex Hormozi's nine writing tips, and Papa Kojo's own feedback.

The trigger for this file, in his words (2026-11-16): "Most people don't run out of money on a big day. They run out on the small ones." He could tell at once it was AI. The giveaway was the flip: a common idea set up, then turned over. It is everywhere in AI writing, so readers now spot it in one line.

No single tell proves anything. Readers react to the pile-up. Two or three of these in one short post and the whole thing smells machine-made.

## 1. Shape tells (the worst ones)

| Tell | Looks like | Do this instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The flip / negative parallelism | "It's not X. It's Y." "X isn't about A, it's about B." "Most people think X. But actually Y." "Not because..., but because..." "Y rather than X." | Say the true thing straight. Or show two real pictures next to each other and let the reader feel the gap. Never write the "not". |
| Sweeping opener | "Most people...", "We all...", "Everyone knows...", "In a world where..." | Start with one person, one moment, one fact. |
| Label, then reveal | "The trouble is...", "The key is...", "The secret is...", "Here's the thing:", "The truth is..." | Delete the label and say the point. |
| Colon reveal | "The answer: consistency." | Write a normal sentence. |
| Rhetorical question answered at once | "Why does this happen? Because..." | Ask only questions you leave open for a while, or none. |
| Tidy triples | "You wobbled, you fell, and you got back on." "Clarity, consistency and courage." | Two things, or four, or one. Three only when there really are three, and not twice in a piece. |
| Stacked fragments | "Simple. Clear. Done." "No hype. No fluff." | Join them into one sentence with "and" or a comma. |
| Moral at the end of every paragraph | Each paragraph closes on a punchy one-liner | Let most paragraphs just end. One strong line per piece is plenty. |
| The bow on top | "And that's okay." "The rest will follow." "The feeling will catch up." "That's the real lesson." | Stop one sentence earlier. End on a concrete thing or a small action. |
| -ing tail clauses | "..., showing how much it matters." "..., highlighting the need for..." | Cut the tail, or make it its own plain sentence with a real subject. |
| Everything balanced | Every paragraph the same length, every sentence the same shape | Let one paragraph be one line and another be four. Let one sentence run on. |

## 2. Word tells

Wikipedia's editors list words that appear far more in AI text than in human text since 2023: additionally, align with, boasts (meaning "has"), bolstered, crucial, deep dive, delve, emphasizing, enduring, enhance, fostering, garner, highlight (verb), interplay, intricate, landscape (abstract), meticulous, pivotal, robust, showcase, tapestry, testament, underscore (verb), valuable, vibrant. Add Papa Kojo's own banned list in voice-profile.md, plus: seamless, elevate, empower, resonate, profound, nuanced, holistic, quietly, genuinely, truly, deeply, powerful.

Fancy swaps are also a tell. Humans write "used", "tried", "moved", "died", "wrote". AI writes "utilized", "attempted", "relocated", "passed away", "authored".

AI also avoids plain "is" and "has" ("serves as", "stands as", "boasts"). Use "is" and "has" freely.

AI also avoids repeating a word, so it rotates synonyms ("the city... the metropolis... the urban centre"). Humans repeat the word. Repeat it.

## 3. Punctuation tells

- Em dashes: banned for him. But note what Wikipedia found in 2026: models told never to use em dashes now use only full stops and commas, and that sameness is itself a tell.
- So use the full range a person uses when talking: a question mark when you wonder, brackets for a side comment (sparingly), a colon for a real list, a semicolon almost never. Contractions everywhere they'd be spoken.
- No exclamation marks in this voice. No emojis unless he asks. No bold inside sentences.

## 4. Content tells

- Claims of significance: "This matters more than ever." "It speaks to something bigger." Cut.
- Vague crowd sources: "experts say", "studies show", "many believe". Name the source or drop it.
- Superficial analysis: a list of nice-sounding reasons with nothing checkable in any of them.
- Fake specifics: invented numbers, invented stories, invented quotes. Worse than vagueness.
- Mind-reading: "You're probably thinking..." "We've all been there."
- Over-hedging and over-reassuring: "It's completely normal to feel...", "And that's okay."

## 5. What human writing has that AI usually lacks

From the same research, plus Harry Dry and Dan Koe:

- **Things you can see.** Harry Dry: "Can I visualize it?" A charging pit bull, not "a better way". Zoom in until there's an object you could drop on your foot.
- **Things you can check.** "Can I falsify it?" "He looks like Ryan Gosling" beats "he's handsome". A fact can be a place, a name, a price, a date. The adjective is the failure; the fact is the fix.
- **Things only you could say.** "Can nobody else say this?" If a stranger could sign it, it's wallpaper.
- **Plain grammar.** "There is", "it has", "I tried", "very", "probably", "kind of". Humans use small qualifiers and simple verbs.
- **Definite statements.** "This is the only..." "It was the first..." Humans commit.
- **Uneven rhythm.** Real writing breathes unevenly. One sentence runs on with "and" because the thought did. The next is four words.
- **Sentences that start with And, But, So.** Harry Dry's rule 5: it flows better.
- **A little self-awareness.** Dan Koe: "I'm actually quite boring (which I like)." "I'm not the best podcast guest yet." A real person admits limits.
- **Repetition on purpose.** Saying the same word again, or returning to the same idea in later pieces (Dan Koe's "inner album of greatest hits").
- **Logic chains instead of flips.** Hormozi at his most natural: "You would be richer if you did more of fewer things because when you do fewer things, you only do the things with the highest return. And when you do more of things with the highest return, you make more." This is because → and → which means. It explains instead of flipping, and it sounds like a person thinking out loud.

## 6. The kitchen table test

Harry Dry: read it aloud at the kitchen table. If your partner cringes, rewrite it. For Papa Kojo: would he say this sentence, in this order, to his wife or a friend in Kumasi? If any line would make them look up and say "who talks like that?", it goes.
