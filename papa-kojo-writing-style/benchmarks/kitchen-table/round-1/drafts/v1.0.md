## B04
I reviewed 150 designer portfolios for a project called Portfolio Field Notes. A few patterns kept showing up.

The strongest portfolios explained the problem before showing any visuals. By the time I saw the design, I already knew what it was meant to solve, so I could judge whether it worked.

The weaker ones were galleries of images with no context. Some of the work may well have been good, but I couldn't tell what the designer was thinking or what the project needed.

Several portfolios also made it hard to find contact details. That seems small, but it's the step that turns interest into a conversation. If someone likes your work and can't find how to reach you, you may lose them right there.

So if you're updating your portfolio, start each project with a few lines on the problem: who it was for, what wasn't working and what you set out to change. Then show the visuals. And put your contact details where nobody has to search for them.

## B05
A shop can be full of customers every day and still struggle to pay its supplier at the end of the month. Being busy and having cash are two different things.

Part of the reason is stock bought on credit. The goods arrive today, but the bill comes later. While that stock sits on the shelf or sells slowly, the debt is already counting down. When the supplier's date comes, the money from those sales may not be in your hands yet.

The other part is customers who pay late. A sale on credit looks like income in your book, but you can't pay rent or restock with a promise. Until the customer pays, that money is still in their pocket.

Put the two together and you get a squeeze. You owe money on a fixed date, while the money owed to you arrives whenever the customer is ready. The shop looks busy because it is busy. The problem is the timing.

So before asking how to sell more, it helps to ask a quieter question. After a cedi leaves your shop, how long does it take to come back? That gap is what this issue is about.

## B06
In Exodus 20:8, the Lord says, "Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy."

I want to point to the first word: remember. We usually need reminding about the things that are easy to forget. Maybe the Lord knew that when life gets busy, the Sabbath would be one of the first things we let slip.

Many of us have seasons when work doesn't stop. There are deadlines, customers, messages and tasks we didn't finish on Saturday. It's easy to tell ourselves we'll just reply to a few emails on Sunday, or do a little work after church. None of these choices feels big on its own. But little by little, Sunday can start to look like every other day.

Keeping the Sabbath holy asks us to set the day apart on purpose. That might mean finishing what we can before Sunday, telling people when we'll be available again, or putting the phone away during the sacrament. It also means filling the day with things that bring us closer to the Lord and to each other, and not only stopping work.

This week, I invite us to look at our plans before Saturday night. What can we finish early? What can honestly wait until Monday? When we prepare ahead to remember the Sabbath, we give the Lord a day that truly belongs to Him.

## B10
A salary gives you something a new business rarely gives at the start: money that arrives every month. That is the strongest reason for a young person in Ghana to start a side business while still employed.

When your rent and food are covered, the business doesn't have to pay you right away. You can test an idea with a few customers, learn what people will actually pay for and make mistakes while they're still cheap. Someone who quits first has to make the business work fast, and that pressure can push them into bad decisions, like borrowing too early or taking any customer at any price.

But a side business costs something too. It takes evenings, weekends and energy. It competes with rest, family, church and the learning you may need to grow in your job. If you're tired every morning, your work can slip, and your employer will notice before your business pays enough to replace your salary.

The real objection is this: some businesses need full attention. If your customers want you during working hours, or the idea depends on moving faster than competitors, half your time may give you half a business. I think that's a fair point. For some ideas, a side business is just a slow way to fail.

Still, many ideas don't fail for lack of effort. They fail because nobody wanted them at that price. A side business lets you find that out before you give up your income.

There are times when it may not be wise. If you've just started your job and are still learning it, your energy may be better spent there. If your contract doesn't allow outside work, or the business would compete with your employer, the risk isn't worth it. And if the business needs borrowed money while you have no savings, a bad month at the business can quickly become a bad month at home.

So my honest view is that it can make sense, but only if you start small, test with real customers and decide in advance when you'll either stop or commit fully. A side business should teach you something. If it only makes you tired, it's time to look at it again.
