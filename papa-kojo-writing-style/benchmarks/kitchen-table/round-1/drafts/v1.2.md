## B04
I reviewed 150 designer portfolios for a project called Portfolio Field Notes. A few patterns kept coming up.

The strongest portfolios explained the problem before showing any visuals. By the time I got to the designs, I knew what each one was meant to solve, so I could judge whether it worked.

The weaker ones were galleries of images with no context. Some of the work may well have been good, but I couldn't tell what the brief was or what choices the designer had made along the way.

Several portfolios also made it hard to find contact details. That sounds like a small thing, but it has a real cost. If someone likes your work and can't reach you, the portfolio stops right there.

So if you're updating yours, here's one change worth making. Open each project with a short note on the problem you were trying to solve, then show the work. And put your email or phone number somewhere a visitor can find it without searching.

## B05
A shop can be busy from opening to closing and still be short of cash when a supplier comes to collect.

That sounds strange at first. If people are buying every day, where is the money going? Very often it is tied up in two places.

The first is stock bought on credit. The goods arrive, you sell some of them, and the supplier's bill is due on a fixed date. If the stock hasn't all sold by then, you pay for it with money you had set aside for something else, like rent or the next order.

The second is customers who pay late. A sale on credit still counts as a sale, but there is no cash in your hand yet. When several customers owe you at the same time, the shop can look healthy while the drawer stays thin.

Put the two together and you have money going out on the supplier's schedule and coming in on the customer's schedule. When those dates don't line up, even a busy shop runs short.

So a useful question for any owner is when the money actually arrives and when it has to leave. In the rest of this issue, I'll look at simple ways to keep track of that gap.

## B06
Exodus 20:8 says, "Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy."

The first word in that verse is "remember." The Lord seems to expect that we could forget. When work is busy, that's easy to understand. There are messages to answer, deadlines waiting on Monday, and the thought that one more hour on Sunday would help us catch up.

Keeping the day holy asks us to decide before Sunday arrives. If we wait until Sunday morning to make the choice, it becomes very easy for work to win. So it may help to plan the week with the Sabbath already in it. We can finish what we're able to on Saturday, set aside what can wait, and let the people we work with know when we won't be available.

That won't make every week easy. Some of us have jobs that can't simply stop for a day, and each of us has to work that out honestly with the Lord. But the commandment still gives us something clear to aim for. It asks us to set apart one day when our attention turns to Him and to worship, and away from the list of things we haven't finished.

I invite each of us to look at the coming week and decide now how we'll remember the Sabbath, before the busy days begin.

## B10
Should a young person in Ghana start a side business while they still have a job? I think it can be a good idea, but only under the right conditions.

The main reason in favour is the salary. A steady income means the business doesn't have to pay your rent in its first months. You can test prices, make mistakes and learn what customers actually want without putting your home or your food at risk. A job can also teach useful skills, and some of the people you meet at work may become your first customers.

A second reason is that one source of income can be fragile. If you lose your job, a small business that already has some customers gives you something to stand on while you look for the next step.

The case against is mostly about time and energy. A job takes most of your day. A business needs attention in the evenings and at weekends, and that time has to come from somewhere, usually from rest, family or church. When you're stretched thin, both the job and the business can suffer. Some employers also have rules about outside work, so it's worth reading your contract before you start.

The strongest objection is that a side business never gets a fair chance. If it only gets what is left after a long day at work, it may stay small for years, and you might decide the idea was bad when it was really underfed. Some people argue that you should commit fully or leave it alone.

There's truth in that. But committing fully without savings is a big risk for someone early in their working life. A middle path is to treat the side business as a test with a clear question: will people pay for this, and how much? Once you have a real answer, you can decide whether it deserves more of your time.

There are also times when it may not be wise at all. If the business can only start with borrowed money and you have no savings, waiting may be better. If it competes with your employer or relies on their clients, it could cost you the job. And if you're in a new role that still needs most of your attention, or your health or family already need more of you, a year of saving and planning may be the better start.

So my honest view is yes, often, as long as you start small and you're clear about what you want to learn from it.
