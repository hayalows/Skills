## B04
I reviewed 150 designer portfolios for a project I'm calling Portfolio Field Notes. A few patterns kept showing up.

The strongest portfolios explained the problem before they showed any visuals. By the time I got to the screens, I already knew what they were meant to fix. That made it much easier to judge whether the design worked.

The weaker ones were galleries of images with no context. I could see the work, but I couldn't tell what the brief was, who it was for or what choices the designer made along the way.

Several portfolios also made it hard to find contact details. It's a small thing, but if someone likes your work and wants to reach you, they shouldn't have to go looking.

If you're a designer, here's one change worth trying. Before each project, write two or three plain sentences about the problem you were solving. Then show the work. It gives the reader a reason to look closely at what comes next.

## B05
A shop can have customers coming in every day and still be short of cash at the end of the month. It sounds strange, but it happens often enough, and two causes explain a lot of it.

The first is stock bought on credit. The goods are on your shelves and you're selling them, but the supplier still has to be paid. If the payment date comes before enough of that stock has sold, the money has to come from somewhere else. A busy week can hide this, because sales feel strong while the bill is still waiting.

The second is customers who pay late. You've handed over the goods and the sale is in your book, but the cash isn't in your hands yet. Your rent and your supplier won't wait for it. So the business can look fine on paper while the cash box stays thin.

Put the two together and you get a gap. Money goes out on fixed dates, and money comes in when customers decide to pay. Being busy doesn't close that gap. It can even make it wider, because more sales on credit means more of your money is sitting with other people.

In this issue, I want to look at how you can spot that gap early.

## B06
In Exodus 20:8 the Lord says, "Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy." I'd like to start with that word, "remember."

When work is busy, the Sabbath is one of the first things to get squeezed. There's always another task to finish or another message to answer before Monday. None of these things are wicked on their own. But little by little, they can turn Sunday into a lighter workday, and we lose the rest and worship the day is meant to give us.

The commandment begins with "remember," and to me that suggests we need to think about the Sabbath before it arrives. If the week is full, we can try to finish what we can on Saturday, so Sunday isn't spent catching up. We can decide ahead of time what we will and won't do that day, instead of deciding when we're tired and under pressure.

Keeping the day holy also means filling it with good things. We come to sacrament meeting and take the sacrament. We can give some of our time to the Lord, to our families and to people who need us. Those are the things work can crowd out if we don't make room for them.

I'd invite each of us to think of one thing we could change this week to keep the Sabbath better. It may be something small. If we remember the day before it comes, we'll be more ready to keep it holy when it does.

## B10
Should young people in Ghana start a side business while they still have a job? I think it can be a good idea, but only under certain conditions.

The strongest reason for it is the salary. When your rent and food are covered by your job, a small business doesn't have to pay you right away. You can test an idea slowly, learn what customers will pay for, and make mistakes that don't put your home at risk. If you start only after leaving work, you may not have that cushion. The business has to earn from the first month, and that pressure can lead to rushed choices.

A side business can also teach things a job may not. You learn to set prices, sell, keep records and handle a customer who isn't happy. Even if the business closes, you keep those lessons.

But there's a real objection. Time and energy are limited. If you work full days and then spend your evenings and weekends on a business, something gets less attention. Sometimes it's your health or your family. Sometimes it's the job itself. A tired employee who takes business calls at the desk may lose the very salary that made the side business possible. Some employers also have rules about outside work, so read your contract before you start.

So when may it not be wise? If you have no savings and any loss would hurt you badly, it may be better to save first. If the business needs a loan you can't repay from your salary, the risk is no longer small. If your job is new or very demanding, you may need to settle in before taking on more. And if the business competes with your employer, the trouble may cost more than the profit is worth.

On balance, I think a side business is worth trying when it starts small, uses money you can afford to lose, and fits around your job without hurting it. Give it a set number of hours each week and keep simple records. After a few months, those records will show you whether to keep going, change course or stop.
