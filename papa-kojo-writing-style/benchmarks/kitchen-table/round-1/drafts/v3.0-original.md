## B04
I went through 150 designer portfolios for a project called Portfolio Field Notes, and a few things kept showing up.

The strongest portfolios explained the problem before showing any visuals. By the time I reached the screens, I knew what the designer was trying to solve, so I could judge whether the design worked.

The weaker ones were galleries of images with no context. Some of the work may have been good, but I had no way to tell what it was for or why it looked the way it did.

Several also made it hard to find contact details. I had to hunt for an email or a link, and that is a small thing to get wrong when someone is trying to reach you.

If you are updating your portfolio, start each project with two or three lines on the problem you were solving, then show the work. And put your contact details where a busy person can find them in a few seconds.

## B05
A shop can have customers at the counter from morning till evening and still be short of cash at the end of the month. If you run one, you may know this feeling. Sales look good, but when a supplier calls for payment, the money is not there.

In many cases the money was made. It is just stuck in two places.

The first is stock bought on credit. You take goods today and promise to pay the supplier later. The goods sell, but part of the cash from those sales is already owed. When the payment date comes, a busy month can leave you with very little in hand.

The second is customers who pay late. You give them the goods now and they promise to pay next week, and next week turns into next month. On paper you made a sale, but the cash is still with them.

Put the two together and you are paying your suppliers before your customers pay you. That gap is where a busy shop runs short of cash.

In this issue, I want to look at that gap more closely, and at a few simple ways to see it coming before it catches you.

## B06
Brothers and sisters, I want to speak for a few minutes about keeping the Sabbath day holy when work is busy.

In Exodus 20:8, the Lord says, "Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy." I notice that the commandment begins with the word "remember." When work is heavy, Sunday can arrive before we are ready for it. There are still messages to answer and plans to make for Monday. It is easy to come to church in body while our minds are still at work.

I think that is part of why the Lord asks us to remember. Keeping the day holy often starts before Sunday. If we know the week will be busy, we can plan for the Sabbath the way we would plan for an important meeting. We can finish what we can on Saturday, let the people we work with know when we will be available, and put the phone away during sacrament meeting.

Some weeks we will not get it right. A deadline will press on us, or a problem at work will follow us home. The commandment is still there for us the next week, and we can try again.

This week, I invite each of us to choose one thing we will set aside on Sunday so we can give the day to the Lord. It can be a small thing, and a small thing is a good place to start.

## B10
If you have a job in Ghana and an idea on the side, it's fair to ask whether you should start now or wait.

I think starting while you're employed can be wise, mostly because of the salary. A side business usually takes time before it pays. If your job covers rent and food, you can test the idea without needing every sale to feed you. You can start small, learn what customers will pay for, and make mistakes while they are still cheap.

It can also help with the job itself. Running something of your own teaches you about pricing, customers and cash in a way a job may not. And if the job ends one day, you won't be starting from nothing.

But there are good reasons to be careful. A side business takes the evenings and weekends that used to be for rest and family. When you are tired, both the job and the business can suffer, because you are only half present in each. Your employer may also have rules about outside work, so read your contract before you start.

A fair objection is that a business given only spare hours will never grow. There's truth in that. Some businesses need full attention, especially ones where customers expect you to be there during the day. If your idea is like that, doing it on the side may only show you that people want it. It may not grow until you can give it more.

I still think that's worth learning before you leave a salary. Knowing that people will pay for what you offer is a much better place to decide from than a guess.

There are times when it may not be wise. If you have no savings and the business needs you to borrow, the risk can land on your family. If your job is new and you are still learning it, splitting your attention may cost you more than it gives. And if the business would compete with your employer, it could cost you the job.

So my view is that a side business is worth trying when your job is steady, the cost to start is small and you can still protect some time for rest. Start with something you can test over a few weekends, and decide your next step from what you learn.
