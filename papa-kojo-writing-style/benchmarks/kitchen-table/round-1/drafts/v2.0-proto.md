## B04
I reviewed 150 designer portfolios for a project called Portfolio Field Notes. A few patterns kept showing up.

The strongest portfolios explained the problem before showing any visuals. I knew what the designer was trying to fix, so the screens that followed made sense. I could judge the work because I understood the job it had to do.

The weaker ones were galleries of images with no context. Some of the images looked good. But I could not tell what they solved or why the designer made those choices.

Several portfolios also made it hard to find contact details. If someone likes your work and cannot reach you, the portfolio has not done its job.

So if you are a designer updating your portfolio, start each project with two or three lines on the problem you were solving. Then show the visuals. And put your contact details where nobody has to search for them.

## B05
Picture a provisions shop in Kumasi with customers at the counter from morning to evening. The owner is busy all day. Yet when the supplier calls for payment, there is not enough cash in the drawer.

How can a shop that sells every day still be short of money?

Often, two things are happening at once. First, the stock on the shelves was bought on credit. The goods are in the shop, but the money for them is still owed to the supplier, and that bill has a due date.

Second, some customers buy now and pay later. The sale is made, but the cash has not come in. So the owner is waiting to be paid, while the supplier is waiting to be paid by the owner.

When the supplier's date comes before the customers pay, the shop runs short. The shop is busy, and that is real. But busy only tells you goods are moving. It does not tell you cash is in hand.

That gap costs money. You may pay a supplier late or borrow in a hurry just to keep the shelves full.

In this issue, I will help you see this gap early, so you can plan for it before it catches you.

## B06
Brothers and sisters, I would like to share a few thoughts on keeping the Sabbath day holy when work is busy.

Many of us have weeks that feel full. There are reports to finish, customers to serve, school work, and things at home that need our attention. By the time Sunday comes, it can feel like the work is still calling us.

The Lord gave a clear commandment in Exodus 20:8: "Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy."

I notice the first word, remember. The Lord knew we would be busy. He knew the work of the week could make us forget. So He asked us to remember on purpose.

Keeping the Sabbath holy in a busy season may mean planning ahead. We can finish what we can on Saturday. We can put the laptop and the work phone away. We can come to sacrament meeting, take the sacrament, and spend the day with the Lord and with our families.

The work will still be there on Monday. On the Sabbath, we give the Lord our time, and we trust Him with the rest.

I invite each of us to choose one thing we can set aside this Sunday. That small choice can help us remember the Sabbath day and keep it holy.

## B10
Should you start a side business while you still have a job?

For many young people in Ghana, the salary comes at month end and is gone soon after. Rent, transport, data and family needs all take their share. So a second income sounds like a good idea. A side business could mean selling clothes, baking, designing or offering a skill on weekends.

I think it can be a good idea, for a few reasons. Your salary pays the basic bills, so the business does not have to feed you from the first month. That gives you time to learn, make mistakes and test whether people will really pay. You also learn things a job may not teach you, like how to price, how to talk to customers and how to keep records.

But there is a fair objection. A job already takes most of your energy. If you come home tired and spend the evening on orders, something will suffer. It may be your work, your health or your family time. Some employers also do not allow staff to run a business on the side, especially one that competes with them. Breaking that rule can cost you the job that was meant to keep you safe.

That objection is real, and more motivation will not make it go away. The answer is to be honest about your time. If you can give the business only a few hours a week, start something that fits a few hours a week. Keep it small until it proves itself.

There are also times when it may not be wise to start at all. If you are in your first months at a new job and still learning the role, give the job your attention first. If you would need to borrow money to start, think hard, because a loan turns a side business into pressure. And if you have no savings for emergencies, a small cushion may be the better first step.

So yes, I think a side business can help a young worker grow, as long as it starts with clear eyes. Check your contract. Count your hours. Start with money you can afford to lose. Then let the business earn its place before you give it more.
