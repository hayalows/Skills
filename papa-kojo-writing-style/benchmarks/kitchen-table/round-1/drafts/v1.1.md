## B04
What makes me keep scrolling through a designer's portfolio?

I've been asking myself that since I went through 150 of them for a project I call Portfolio Field Notes. I still don't have the full answer, though a few things kept showing up.

The strongest ones told me the problem first. What was broken, and who it hurt. Then came the visuals, and by then I knew what I was looking at.

The weaker ones were galleries. Image after image, with no word on why any of it was made. It felt like being handed a plate of food and nobody telling you what's in it (I'll still eat it, but I can't tell you if the cook did well).

And in several, I had to hunt for an email or a phone number. Some people won't hunt.

If you're a designer, open your portfolio today and pick your best project. Before the first image, write two or three lines on the problem it solved. Then make sure someone can reach you without searching.

## B05
Busy every day. Short of cash every week.

Maybe that's your shop. Both parts are true at the same time, and two things usually sit behind it.

First, the stock on your shelf was bought on credit. The supplier gave it to you, and the supplier wants paying on a set date.

Second, customers buy today and pay later, and some of them pay late. So the money from your sales is still in other people's pockets when the supplier's date comes round.

Picture a house where the rice in the kitchen came from a neighbour on credit, and the family eats from that pot every night, and a cousin who ate there says he'll bring his share next month. The pot looks full. The neighbour still knocks on the day she was promised, and the cousin's share isn't in the house yet.

A shop works the same way. Sales tell you how much moved out the door. Cash tells you how much came back, and when it came.

So your shop can be busy every day and still be short on the one day that counts, the day the supplier comes for his money.

## B06
Brothers and sisters, the Lord gave Israel a short command in Exodus 20:8: "Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy."

I've been thinking about the first word. Why "remember"? He could have said "keep the sabbath day" and stopped there. Maybe He knew that a busy week makes it easy to forget.

When work is busy, Sunday can start to look like a day to catch up. The phone keeps buzzing. There's always one more order or one more email to finish before Monday.

Think of a football match. At half-time the whistle goes, and the players walk off the pitch. Nobody stays out there saying the game is too big to stop. They drink water, they listen to the coach, and then they go back out to the same match.

I don't think there's one rule that fits every job. Some of us work in places where Sunday is hard to protect. But I think remembering starts before Sunday comes. We finish what we can on Saturday. We put the phone away when we walk into this chapel. And when we keep the day holy, I believe the Lord helps us carry the work that's still waiting on Monday, in ways we may not see until much later.

So when the whistle goes this Sunday, may we walk off the pitch, and remember.

## B10
Keep your job while you test the idea. If you're young, employed in Ghana and thinking about a side business, that's where I'd start. I'll try to be honest about where it breaks down too.

The case for it works like a chain. Your salary pays for rent and food, so the business doesn't have to feed you yet. That means you can price fairly, wait for the right customers, and make small mistakes without panic. And because you're not desperate, you have time to learn what people will really pay for.

Think of how a house in Kumasi stores water. You fill the buckets while the tap is still running, so a dry week doesn't stop everything. A side business started while you're paid is a bucket you fill while the tap still runs. If the job ends one day, you have something already in place.

There's a cost, and it's time. A side business eats your evenings and weekends. So you get tired, and when you're tired your work at the job can slip, which means you risk the very salary that was holding the business up.

One real objection is about loyalty. Some employers don't allow side work at all, and some side businesses compete with the employer's own customers. That's a fair worry. Read your contract before you print a single flyer, and if it's unclear, ask.

There are times when it may not be wise. When you have no savings and any loss would mean missing rent. When the job is new and you're still learning it. When your contract says no. When the idea needs full days that you simply don't have.

If none of those fit you, starting small while you're employed is a sensible way to try. If one of them fits, wait a while and fill the bucket another way, with savings.

Your contract and your savings are both things you can check tonight. So is the number of free hours you have in a week.
