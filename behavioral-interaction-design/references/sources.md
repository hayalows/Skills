# Sources and reading map

## Primary source

Stephen P. Anderson. *Seductive Interaction Design: Creating Playful, Fun, and Effective User Experiences*. New Riders, 2011. ISBN 978-0-321-72552-3.

B01–B25 cover the chapters. B26 covers the bridge between the third and fourth sections. Page numbers are **1-based physical PDF pages in the supplied 315-page copy**. For other editions, use the chapter numbers. The design decisions below are interpretations and modern applications. Historical product cases illustrate possibilities. They provide neither current product behavior nor transferable effect estimates.

| ID | Book location | Design question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| B01 | Ch. 1, PDF 21–38 | Does the user lack a reason to act, a usable path, or both? Compare the profile completion prompt and the music-service onboarding example. Distinguish the six qualities of the experience. |
| B02 | Ch. 2, PDF 41–43 | Does the presentation change how someone understands or approaches a problem? Treat the candle task and affect studies as starting points for hypotheses. |
| B03 | Ch. 3, PDF 44–53 | Can people infer relationships, priority, and state from grouping and visual cues? Check which cue creates the inference. |
| B04 | Ch. 4, PDF 54–67 | Does the design change first impressions, perceived wait, perceived ease, or actual performance? Measure these separately. |
| B05 | Ch. 5, PDF 68–75 | What expectations do imagery, metaphor, and word choice bring into the experience? Check unintended associations with the intended audience. |
| B06 | Ch. 6, PDF 77–83 | When do familiarity, utility, audience, and context matter more than polish? Test which novel element the task can accommodate. |
| B07 | Ch. 7, PDF 84–87 | What does adding a face imply about authorship, trust, or belonging? Verify the implication. |
| B08 | Ch. 8, PDF 90–98 | When does warmth fit the moment? The MailChimp case provides a historical example of tone and timing. |
| B09 | Ch. 9, PDF 99–114 | Where could a useful surprise or gift sit after a predictable task? Separate the Dopplr recap example from reward schedules and reciprocity pressure. |
| B10 | Ch. 10, PDF 115–120 | Can a pattern prompt optional exploration or make information easier to organize? Protect required meaning from novelty. |
| B11 | Ch. 11, PDF 121–130 | What specific question would the user want answered? Compare the value of the answer with the cost of reaching it. |
| B12 | Ch. 12, PDF 131–134 | Does a personal label, configuration, or creation support identity or useful organization? Keep the choice reversible and privacy-aware. |
| B13 | Ch. 13, PDF 137–150 | Can planning, a small first step, sequencing, or visible progress turn an intention into action? Distinguish assistance from escalating commitment. |
| B14 | Ch. 14, PDF 151–162 | Can grouping, progressive disclosure, or a simpler question cut work without hiding the decision? |
| B15 | Ch. 15, PDF 163–168 | What gets noticed first and what is accidentally or deliberately de-emphasized? Inspect the decision from the user's side. |
| B16 | Ch. 16, PDF 169–184 | What does inaction do? Check defaults, suggestions, personal relevance, and ownership cues for risk and reversibility. |
| B17 | Ch. 17, PDF 185–193 | How do reference points, wording, and personalization shape interpretation? Test understanding of real costs and tradeoffs. |
| B18 | Ch. 18, PDF 194–200 | If the interface were a dialogue, what would the next natural exchange be? Role-play, script, split requests, defer options, and examine small moments. |
| B19 | Ch. 19, PDF 207–212 | Is there voluntary play and a challenge? Look at choices, feedback, goals, rewards, and the setting before adding game furniture. |
| B20 | Ch. 20, PDF 214–230 | What activity would people like to get better at? Match challenge to skill and use successive questions to find what matters. |
| B21 | Ch. 21, PDF 232–246 | Is the constraint real or chosen? Does it improve the decision or create pressure? Consider capacity, duration, and access separately. |
| B22 | Ch. 22, PDF 247–259 | Does feedback help the user choose a next step? Can somebody improve the number while harming the outcome? |
| B23 | Ch. 23, PDF 261–272 | What does the milestone recognize? Keep performance goals meaningful and keep optional narrative separate from task information. |
| B24 | Ch. 24, PDF 273–277 | Which ideas meet expectations, improve performance, or add unexpected value? Test for indifference before investing. |
| B25 | Ch. 25, PDF 278–290 | What is the type of behavior, the moment of the prompt, the level of effort, and the user's reason to act? Check audience differences and test in context. |
| B26 | Interlude after Ch. 18, PDF 201–204 | Which observable actions connect a business goal to a user benefit? Generate several paths and identify who gains and who carries the cost. |

## Research and standards

Use these for mechanism context and limitations. Start with the linked paper, abstract, or issuing body before making a precise claim. Historical lab studies and broad reviews serve different purposes. Use product observations to choose the intervention and a suitable local test to estimate the effect.

| ID | Reference | What it supports and how to use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| R01 | Kurosu & Kashimura (1995), [Apparent usability vs. inherent usability](https://doi.org/10.1145/223355.223680) | Separate perceived ease from actual task performance. |
| R02 | Lindgaard, Fernandes, Dudek & Brown (2006), [Rapid visual impressions of websites](https://doi.org/10.1080/01449290500330448) | Study first visual judgments separately from trust, comprehension, or conversion. The exposure duration is a study condition. |
| R03 | Moshagen, Musch & Göritz (2009), [Visual aesthetics and performance](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19787509/) | The experiment offers grounds to test performance in addition to preference. Use task and context limits. |
| R04 | Sonderegger & Sauer (2010), [Aesthetics in usability testing](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19892317/) | Appearance can matter during a usability test. Control for visual treatment and interpret device-specific findings in context. |
| R05 | Hekkert, Snelders & van Wieringen (2003), [Typicality and novelty in industrial design](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12648393/) | Compare the familiarity and novelty of a design with the intended audience. |
| R06 | Loewenstein (1994), [The psychology of curiosity](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/PsychofCuriosity.pdf) | The information-gap account helps frame specific questions and test payoff. |
| R07 | Freedman & Fraser (1966), [Foot-in-the-door technique](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5969145/) | Recognize how a small prior ask may influence a later one. Keep the later choice independent and easy to refuse. |
| R08 | Nunes & Drèze (2006), [Endowed progress effect](https://doi.org/10.1086/500480) | Test whether transparent recognition of a genuine head start helps persistence. |
| R09 | Iyengar & Lepper (2000), [When choice is demotivating](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11138768/) | Larger assortments can create friction in some conditions. |
| R10 | Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (2010), [Choice overload meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1086/651235) and Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman (2015), [Choice overload and moderators](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002) | The average effect and moderators differ across studies. Check option complexity, decision difficulty, preference certainty, and the task goal before shrinking a set. |
| R11 | Johnson & Goldstein (2003), [Do Defaults Save Lives?](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14631022/) | Defaults affect choice. Treat an active decision as the safe starting point for consequential settings. |
| R12 | Tversky & Kahneman (1981), [Framing of decisions](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7455683/) | Equivalent outcomes can produce different choices when presented differently. Test balanced comprehension. |
| R13 | Ryan & Deci (2000), [Intrinsic and extrinsic motivations](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10620381/) | Separate enjoyment of the activity from incentives for doing it. |
| R14 | Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), [Rewards and intrinsic motivation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/) | Effects vary with reward type and contingency. Watch voluntary return when the reward ends. |
| R15 | Kluger & DeNisi (1996), [Feedback interventions and performance](https://teach.peabody.kids/library/studies/kluger-denisi-1996) | Effects vary and can be negative. Test task improvement and make feedback actionable. The linked source summarizes the original meta-analysis. |
| R16 | Kano, Seraku, Takahashi & Tsuji (1984), [Attractive quality and must-be quality](https://doi.org/10.20684/quality.14.2_147) | Use paired reactions to presence and absence to inform categorization. |
| R17 | Fogg (2009), [A behavior model for persuasive design](https://doi.org/10.1145/1541948.1541999) | Use motivation, ability, and a trigger as interacting conditions in a particular moment. Treat the model as a diagnostic framework. |
| R18 | U.S. Federal Trade Commission (2022), [Bringing Dark Patterns to Light](https://www.ftc.gov/reports/bringing-dark-patterns-light) | A practical source for identifying misleading terms, obstructed cancellation, and interface pressure. |
| R19 | W3C, [Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) | Check relevant success criteria and evaluate the real interface with appropriate assistive technology. |
| R20 | Isen, Daubman & Nowicki (1987), [Positive affect and creative problem solving](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3598858/) | Lab evidence can motivate a hypothesis about affect and performance. Keep cognitive demands and emotional context specific to the product. |
