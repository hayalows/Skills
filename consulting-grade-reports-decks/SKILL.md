# Consulting-Grade Reports & Decks

Use this skill as the research, argument and decision layer for a report or deck. Use premium-editorial-report-pdf for editorial layout when relevant; avoid imposing two competing design systems. Build credibility through a disciplined argument, clear evidence, and useful actions, not through another firm’s branding or surface appearance.

## Start with the decision

1. Identify the audience, decision, central puzzle, evidence available, risks, and action expected from the reader.
2. Use what the user has provided. Ask one focused question only if the answer would materially change the audience, format, brand, or core decision.
3. State what cannot be proved from the available evidence. Never invent financial results, statistics, citations, quotations, client outcomes, or market facts.
4. Select the deliverable:
   - Use a report when it will be read in detail, cited, or used for reference.
   - Use a deck when it will be presented, discussed, pitched, or scanned quickly.
   - Create both only when the same audience genuinely needs both formats.

For technical document production, use the relevant report, PDF, presentation, document, or spreadsheet workflow available in the environment. Render and visually inspect the final file before delivery.

## Keep an evidence ledger

For each material claim, record the source or supplied file location, observation period, retrieval date when relevant, units, method and limitations. Distinguish facts, calculations, assumptions and recommendations. Reconcile contradictory sources before writing a confident headline. Keep enough input data and calculation detail to reproduce important numbers.

Use scenarios or ranges when the evidence cannot support a point estimate. Explain which assumption changes the decision. Never infer causation from a simple correlation or present an illustrative framework as validated research. A missing evidence base may justify a clearly labeled provisional memo, not a polished invented report.

## Build the argument

Plan the whole work before designing it.

1. Open with an executive summary that states the thesis, key evidence, implications, and the decision in miniature.
2. Give each major section one load-bearing role in the argument. Do not create a loose collection of topics.
3. Start each section with the relevant question, finding or decision. Use a tension or unexpected claim only when the evidence supports it.
4. Give the reader a short, memorable framework when it makes the insight easier to understand or reuse. Define it plainly and do not force a name where the idea is weak.
5. Prove the claim with one or two focused pieces of evidence. Use named examples, a concrete historical analogy, or a real operating situation where they clarify the point.
6. Include an exception, limitation, or condition that could change the conclusion. Credibility falls when every point sounds certain.
7. Transition to the next question. End with actions split by the people who must take them.

## Design discipline

1. Adapt the palette, typography, photography, and graphic elements to the user’s brand. Do not use another organisation’s logo, proprietary assets, distinctive layouts, or visual identity.
2. Use one strong accent colour, near-black text, white space, and greys for secondary information. Do not introduce a second accent colour unless the user’s brand needs it for a clear functional reason.
3. Use the established brand type system. Without one, an editorial serif and clean sans-serif are a useful default, not a requirement.
4. Use oversized section numerals, pull-stat callouts, declarative chart headlines, restrained two-colour charts, and numbered framework boxes only when they clarify the message.
5. Make every chart title state the finding, not the topic. Label important values directly and add readable source or method notes.
6. Use photography as a chapter pause or supporting evidence, not as wallpaper. When no appropriate image exists, use a quiet geometric or colour-field treatment.

## Choose the right structure

### For a report

- Use a front-loaded executive summary, chapter openers, detailed reasoning, citations/footnotes, data pages, and audience-segmented recommendations.
- Use a statement → evidence → implication rhythm on each page.
- Keep one main idea per page where practical, but let detailed analysis breathe when it needs more than a slide’s worth of context.

### For a deck

- Build a 16:9 presentation narrative, not report pages placed on slides.
- Give every slide one dominant message and a takeaway headline.
- Keep body copy short. Place supporting explanation, sources, caveats, and transition language in speaker notes when useful.
- Use the slide type that best communicates the idea: cover, executive summary, section divider, big statement, statistic, chart, comparison, problem and evidence, process, case study, recommendation, or closing.

## Final quality bar

Before delivery, check that:

- The executive summary gives the reader the argument in miniature; it may follow a cover.
- Each major section advances one coherent thesis and contains a meaningful nuance or limitation.
- Every chart and large statistic has a source or states the limits of its evidence.
- The conclusion gives the decision or audience-specific actions, with ownership, dependencies and timing where known. Do not invent owners or dates.
- The brand treatment is original and consistent.
- The rendered file has no overflow, small unreadable text, crowded layouts, weak alignment, low-quality images, unclear charts, or competing ideas on one slide.
